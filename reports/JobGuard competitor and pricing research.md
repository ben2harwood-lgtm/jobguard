# Price at market, let results signal quality

JobGuard should launch close to the founder's test points, with a stronger top tier. The recommended prices are **£29, £59 and £119 a month (ex VAT)**, charged per business rather than per person, with unlimited users and tiers set by how many jobs are live at once. The £59 middle plan should be the one most builders choose. Quoting stays free forever. The trial runs through the builder's first live job instead of a 14-day clock. Annual billing is offered at "two months free". The separate **10% success fee** stays exactly as proposed. The evidence supports this in three ways. First, £29–£59 sits squarely in the range UK small-trade software already charges, about **£25–£45 per user per month**. Second, no competitor found sells small builders a missed-extras check after the final bill, a supplier-overcharge check, or a success fee. Third, 10% is at or below the bottom of almost every "we only get paid if you get paid" rate a small business will know: debt collectors take **8–15%** on fresh debts, claims firms **15–30%** and recovery auditors **20–30%**. The founder's idea that a higher price signals higher quality is real but weak. The average effect is small, it has shrunk over time, and it is weakest for services and for buyers who already know the category, which describes builders who use Tradify or a Checkatrade tool. So JobGuard should look premium through its top tier, by refusing to discount, and by showing the evidence behind what it found, not by raising the entry price. The biggest pricing risk is not a number. It is the hidden final check. If builders come to believe JobGuard knew about a missed extra mid-job and stayed quiet to earn 10%, the research on betrayal and fairness predicts a collapse of trust in a trade that runs on word of mouth. Three weaknesses in the evidence matter here. Most of the behavioural research comes from consumer lab studies. Several competitor prices are third-party or unverified. And no public data measures how much small builders lose to unbilled extras. So the subscription has to pay its own way, and the success fee should be treated as upside until pilot data exist.

## No rival sells a final check, an overcharge check or a success fee to small builders

Every mainstream tool covers the basic flow of quote, job, invoice and card payment, and most now chase unpaid quotes and invoices automatically. JobGuard's distinctive features are thinly covered or absent:

- **Variations** are usually locked behind a middle or top tier: Joist's Elite plan and above ([Joist](https://www.joist.com/pricing/)), Buildertrend's Advanced tier, WorkflowMax's Pro "quote variations" ([WorkflowMax](https://www.workflowmax.com/pricing)), and Fergus Professional's job phases ([Fergus UK](https://fergus.com/uk/pricing/)).
- **Supplier-bill capture** exists as an upsell: Tradify's AI bill reader is in the £44 Plus plan ([Tradify UK](https://www.tradifyhq.com/uk/pricing)), and Powered Now's receipt scanner is in Premium ([Powered Now](https://www.powerednow.com/pricing)).
- **Bill checking** only compares a supplier's bill with the builder's own purchase order. It is found in ApprovalMax's Advanced plan ([ApprovalMax](https://www.approvalmax.com/pricing/approvalmax-for-xero)) and Planyard's Professional plan ([Planyard](https://www.planyard.com/pricing)). No product found checks a supplier invoice against quoted or expected prices.
- **Final accounts**, in the builder's sense of original quote plus approved variations, were not marketed by any small-trade tool reviewed.
- **UK payment notices under the Construction Act** appear only in suites built for the subcontract chain: Site Samurai, Construction AI and Payapps ([Site Samurai](https://www.sitesamurai.co.uk/compare/best-applications-for-payment-software-uk); [Payapps](https://www.payapps.com/uk/)).
- **Voice features** at incumbents mostly mean AI receptionists that answer calls, not capturing the scope of a job by talking through it on site.

The closest products to JobGuard's "find the money you missed" idea each come at it from a different direction:

- **Buildbite** captures changes as they happen on site. It reports a **30% lift in change-order revenue**, which is a self-reported figure, but it charges per user, not on results ([Buildbite](https://buildbite.com/insights/ai-construction-tools)).
- **Trunk Tools** spots missed scope in drawings for enterprise clients, on quote-only contracts (same source).
- **FairRate** audits trade invoices against local rates for **£0.99 per audit**, but it works for the homeowner checking the builder, the opposite direction to JobGuard ([FairRate](https://www.fairrate.ai/)).

This gap analysis has a limit. It rests on pricing and feature pages, Simpro's UK pages could not be read, and "not found" is not proof that a feature does not exist.

A builder trying to assemble similar coverage today would stack several products: a voice-quote app (£15–£59), Tradify Plus (£44 per user), Dext (about £24–£30) and a payment-application tool such as Site Samurai (£99 and up). That comes to roughly **£180–£230 a month for a single user** before any bill-matching add-on, and it still lacks the missed-extras check. The stack is a useful comparison for a pricing page. It is not how builders actually think, though: their real reference point is the single £30–£45 app they already know.

No trade or construction software found takes a percentage of recovered money. The only percentage charges are card-processing fees, such as Fergus's **1.6% + 20p** ([Fergus UK](https://fergus.com/uk/pricing/)), plus Checkatrade's lead fees. So JobGuard's 10% has no precedent in the category. Builders will judge it against outside firms that are paid only when money comes in. On that comparison, 10% looks modest:

Three points follow from the table.

- **Small recoveries.** Consultants on day rates make recovering a £500–£3,000 missed variation uneconomic. At 10%, JobGuard would take £50–£300 on those sums, which opens a recovery route that does not currently exist for small jobs.
- **Withheld payments from homeowners.** 10% matches what a collector charges on fresh debt. But the Late Payment Act's interest and fixed compensation apply only to debts owed by businesses ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/late-commercial-payments-interest-debt-recovery)), so a builder chasing a homeowner bears the whole fee. Collectors' percentages also exclude court and enforcement costs, so JobGuard should state clearly whether escalation is included.
- **Combined models.** Subscription plus success fee is not unheard of. Chaser pairs credit-control software with a separate collections service ([Chaser](https://www.chaserhq.com/blog/debt-collection-software)). Competitors now sell flat-fee alternatives to Paddle Retain's percentage model ([SubRevival](https://subrevival.com/alternatives/profitwell-retain-alternative)), which suggests customers resent percentage fees once the recovered sums grow.

## A higher price buys little credibility; structure and framing do more work

### The founder's price-signals-quality hunch is true but small

The founder's hypothesis has academic support, but the effect is modest. A meta-analysis of 54 studies found a statistically significant but small link between price and perceived quality, with an average correlation of **0.12** ([Rao & Monroe 1989](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224378902600309)). A later review found the effect **has weakened over time and is weaker for services, for durable goods and for buyers familiar with the product** ([Völckner & Hofmann 2007](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/the-price-perceived-quality-relationship-a-meta-analytic-review-a/)). The striking lab results come from consumer products:

- People who paid a discounted price for an energy drink solved fewer puzzles ([Shiv, Carmon & Ariely 2005](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.2005.42.4.383)).
- The same wine tasted better when it was labelled as more expensive ([Plassmann et al. 2008](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0706929105)).

No study was found on price as a quality signal for small-business or trade software. The honest reading is that a price signal helps most when quality is hard to judge before buying, which is true of a new "profit watchdog". That argues against a toy-like price of £9–£15 that would put JobGuard alongside cheap voice-quote gadgets. It does not justify pushing the entry price above the market. Builders know what "an app" costs, and once they have used JobGuard, what it actually finds will outweigh anything the price suggested. JobGuard also has a stronger quality signal than price: a fee it earns only when the builder is paid tells the buyer that JobGuard expects to deliver.

### Three tiers work because people pick the middle, not because of decoys

The dependable effect behind the "good, better, best" layout is the **compromise effect**: people drift toward the middle option because it is easiest to justify. One literature review reports that middle options average **about 17.5% more share** than the other options in the range, a secondary figure ([Manja review of Simonson 1989](https://www.bwl.uni-mannheim.de/media/Lehrstuehle/bwl/Stahl/bachelorstheses/BA_Steven_Manja.pdf)).

The "decoy" effect is much shakier. Dan Ariely's widely quoted Economist subscription example was a classroom demonstration: adding a pointless print-only option moved choices of the bundle from **32% to 84%** ([Decoy effect overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decoy_effect)). Later studies found the effect largely **disappears when options are described realistically** ([Frederick, Lee & Baskin 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmr.12.0061)). In one test only **11 of 91** participants moved toward the target option ([Yang & Lynn 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.14.0020)). The original authors concede it needs clean, easily compared numbers ([Huber, Payne & Puto 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.14.0208)).

For JobGuard, this means every tier should be a real plan someone would actually buy. The plan JobGuard most wants to sell should sit in the middle. The comparison should rest on one or two numbers, such as live jobs, not a long feature grid. The 10% fee should be identical on every tier so it does not blur the comparison.

### £29 beats £30, and nine-endings suit a practical tool

A price ending in 9 feels meaningfully lower **only when the first digit changes**, as with £29 against £30 ([Thomas & Morwitz 2005](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/32/1/54/1796360)). In a real catalogue experiment, the same dress sold **21 units at $39, against 16 at $34 and 17 at $44**. That is a small sample from one clothing item, so it is suggestive rather than conclusive ([Anderson & Simester 2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1023581927405)). Round prices suit purchases made on feeling, while precise prices suit purchases made on reasoning ([Wadhwa & Zhang 2015](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/41/5/1172/2962090)). A profit tool is bought on reasoning, so the evidence does not support a round "£50 looks professional" price. £29, £59 and £119 keep the first-digit advantage.

One question remains open. UK business software is shown ex VAT, but a sole trader below the VAT threshold actually pays £34.80 for a £29 plan. Nobody has tested whether that erodes the effect.

### Charge for live jobs, not people

Pricing experts recommend charging on a measure that grows with the value the customer gets. Most of the popular statistics behind that advice, such as "30% lower churn", trace back to marketing pages rather than published data ([Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/pricing-for-customer-churn-reduction-retention-through-value)). For JobGuard, the success fee already does the job of charging for value. The subscription therefore only needs a simple measure of capacity.

Charging per user would work against the product. JobGuard's evidence comes from people on site, and per-seat pricing discourages builders from adding the site lead or labourers who would feed it photos and notes. Charging by jobs live at the same time tracks workload, is easy to understand and scales from sole traders to 20-person firms. It carries one risk: builders might keep small jobs off the system to stay in a cheaper tier. That would cut JobGuard's detections and its fee income, so job allowances should be generous. When a builder goes over the limit, the product should suggest upgrading next month rather than blocking the job.

### Let the trial run until the first job is live

The benchmarks are consistent on the trade-offs between trial types:

- **Conversion rates:** among B2B products, trials that require a card convert **25–35%** of starters, trials without a card **4–6%**, and freemium plans **3–5%**, counting "good" performers only.
- **Paying customers per 1,000 website visitors:** a card-required trial produced **10.5**, against **3.6** for a trial without a card and **5** for freemium ([ChartMogul 2026](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)).
- **Caveat on the sample:** these are software firms selling to sophisticated buyers, and an earlier survey gives different ranges ([Lenny's Newsletter 2023](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-free-to-paid-conversion)).

On trial length, randomised field experiments point to an optimum rather than "longer is better". One found **7 days beat 14 and 30 days**, adding 5.59% more subscriptions ([Yoganarasimhan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.13420)). Another found 7 days beat 3, lifting later conversions by about 42%, although the absolute gains were small ([Zhang & Duan 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12217587/)). The lesson is that the trial should end soon after the customer first gets real value from the product. JobGuard's value arrives only once a job is live and the watchdog flags something, which can take weeks on a building job. A fixed 14-day clock would usually expire first.

JobGuard already has a natural free layer, because quoting is free by design. That makes a "reverse trial" straightforward: quoting stays free forever, and the watchdog and final account run free on the first live job. A reverse trial gives full features for a limited time and then drops back to the free layer instead of cutting access. No quantitative evidence on its uplift was found ([Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/your-guide-to-reverse-trials)). The moment the builder switches a won job to live is also the natural point to ask for a card, because that is when they commit. Whether asking for a card there helps or hurts is untested.

### Offer annual billing, don't force it

The standard annual discount is **15–20%**, usually presented as "two months free" ([Baremetrics](https://baremetrics.com/blog/annual-vs-monthly-pricing-better-retention)). Powered Now, YourTradebase and Joist all use that wording. Popular claims that annual plans cut churn by 40–60% have no traceable data behind them and mix up cause and effect, because committed customers choose annual plans in the first place ([Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/monthly-vs-annual-billing-how-subscription-length-impacts-saas-churn-and-cash-flow)). Sole traders are short of cash and wary of being locked in, so annual billing should be an option, not the default. Building work is also seasonal, and a winter pause might reduce cancellations more than annual prepayment. That idea has not been tested.

### Frame the price against lost money, but don't overclaim

Presenting a cost as "pennies a day" makes people more willing to pay, because they compare it with small everyday spending ([Gourville 1998](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/24/4/395/1797969)). At £29 a month, "under £1 a day" is credible. At £119 it is not.

Framing a message as a loss rather than a gain has **almost no average effect** in large meta-analyses: correlations of around 0.03–0.04 ([O'Keefe & Jensen 2009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2009.01417.x)). The closest real-world parallel to JobGuard does show promise. Home-energy auditors who showed customers their losses vividly and personally got more of them to act, but loss framing was bundled with other techniques, so its separate effect is unknown ([Gonzales, Aronson & Costanzo 1988](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1559-1816.1988.tb01192.x)). Showing the builder the specific job, photo and supplier line will do more than slogans.

Any claim that "JobGuard typically recovers £X" must wait for real data. The only UK figure on unbilled extras comes from an insurer survey of 408 tradespeople. It says **34% receive no payment for extra work**, but its headline "£2,600 a year" does not add up as published ([Simply Business](https://www.simplybusiness.co.uk/knowledge/trades/unpaid-tasks-costing-uk-tradespeople/)). Late payment, by contrast, is well measured by the government. It costs the UK economy **almost £11bn a year**, causes about **14,000 business closures a year**, and **£26bn** is owed at any one time ([Small Business Commissioner](https://www.smallbusinesscommissioner.gov.uk/late-payments-research-2/)). That makes it the safest hard number for marketing.

### Builders' willingness to pay is anchored low and largely unmeasured

Construction is the **least digitised sector** among UK one-person businesses. Only **29%** use web-based tools or software to run the business, against 53% across all sectors ([GOV.UK Small Business Survey 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/small-business-survey-2024-businesses-with-no-employees/longitudinal-small-business-survey-2024-businesses-with-no-employees)). Many prospects are still on paper, so getting them started may matter more than price for the smallest firms.

Making Tax Digital is now forcing software onto sole traders. It applied from April 2026 to those with income over £50,000, about **860,000 people**, and the threshold falls to £30,000 in 2027 and £20,000 in 2028 ([GoCardless](https://gocardless.com/blog/mtd-itsa-sole-traders-landlords-2026-guide)). That means a second compulsory subscription is competing for the same budget.

Comparison blogs say most sole traders and small teams pay **£15–£40 a month** for job software ([TradePlanr](https://tradeplanr.com/blog/how-much-does-job-management-software-cost)). A widely repeated claim that tradespeople spend £200–£400 a month across eight tools could not be traced to any survey ([Kwowta](https://kwowta.com/best-apps-running-trades-business-uk-2026/)) and should not be used. No robust UK survey of what tradespeople are willing to pay for software exists in the material reviewed.

## The hidden final check, not the 10%, is the pricing risk

Because 10% sits below almost every benchmark, the rate itself is unlikely to be the objection. Research on price fairness says people judge a charge by **who did the work and who bears the cost**, not by the percentage alone ([Xia, Monroe & Cox 2004](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.1.42733)). They also treat exploiting an advantage as unfair, even where passing on genuine costs is accepted ([Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1986](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Justice/Kahneman.pdf)). With a claims firm or a debt collector, the provider does the chasing. With a missed extra, the builder did the physical work and will send the bill, while JobGuard spotted the gap.

Builders will accept a fee where JobGuard visibly did work they could not easily do themselves, such as matching supplier invoices, photos and messages across a whole job. They will reject it where they feel the extra was already in their head or their notes. Showing the work helps. People value a service more when they can see its effort, and that sense of effort creates a feeling that they owe something in return ([Buell & Norton 2011](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Norton_Michael_The%20labor%20illusion%20How%20operational_f4269b70-3732-4fc4-8113-72d0c47533e0.pdf)). This is the case for showing the evidence trail behind each find: which photo, which supplier line, which date. It is also the case for the plan's existing rules that anything the builder logs themselves is always fee-free, and that unclear matches count in the builder's favour.

The deliberate hold-back is the part that needs the most care. Four findings apply:

- **People hate losses caused by someone they trusted more than losses caused by chance.** In trust experiments, people wanted a **54%** chance of a good outcome before trusting another person, against **37%** before taking an equivalent gamble ([Bohnet & Zeckhauser 2004](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=447660)). The finding has been repeated across six countries ([Bohnet et al. 2008](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.98.1.294)).
- **Disclosure does not fix a conflict of interest.** Telling people about a conflict does not neutralise it and can make advisers behave worse ([Cain, Loewenstein & Moore 2005](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=480121)). A line in the terms explaining the hold-back will not remove the objection.
- **Surprise money feels like free money, but only while it is a surprise.** People spend unexpected money more freely than money they anticipated ([Arkes et al. 1994](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597884710636)). If a builder concludes JobGuard "knew on day 12 and told me on day 60", the find stops feeling like a windfall and starts feeling like their own money being held to ransom.
- **Some unbilled work is a deliberate choice.** Tradespeople often do extras free on purpose, hoping for better reviews or recommendations ([Simply Business](https://www.simplybusiness.co.uk/knowledge/trades/unpaid-tasks-costing-uk-tradespeople/)). A reveal that nudges a builder to chase a homeowner for a favour, and then takes a cut, cuts across the builder's own judgement about the customer relationship.

The framing that survives this is a **timing rationale that serves the builder**: "an independent second check after you've done your own bill, like a proofread". The plan's must-surface rules for safety, disputes and work needing prior written agreement are what make that honest. The pricing page should explain the design in one plain sentence, not hide it in the terms. It should also treat "I chose not to charge this" as a fee-free outcome that JobGuard respects, not a lost sale.

How the fee is charged matters too. Mental-accounting research says a small cost absorbed into a larger gain hurts less than the same cost on its own ([Thaler 1985](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/thaler-mktsci1985.pdf)). People also prefer flat charges to a running meter ([Prelec & Loewenstein 1998](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/redblack.pdf); [Lambrecht & Skiera 2006](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.2.212)). JobGuard never holds the builder's customer money, so it cannot literally deduct its fee from the payment. It can, however, show "£1,000 recovered; JobGuard's fee £100; yours £900" on the same screen and statement at the moment the cash is recorded, and bill it promptly. A separate invoice weeks later would feel like an isolated loss.

The proposed model has no offset between the subscription and the fee, so the builder pays twice for one product. That is defensible: even R&D-tax critics draw the "too much" line at contingency fees of 20% or more charged on top of an engagement fee ([Source Advisors](https://sourceadvisors.co.uk/insights/latest-thinking/r-and-d-tax-relief/the-evolution-of-contingent-fees-and-their-place-in-rd-tax-credits-claims/)). But the builder has to see clearly what each payment buys.

Two legal points need a lawyer rather than an inference:

- **Whether this counts as regulated debt collecting or claims management.** On the sources found, helping a builder collect their own invoices is not FCA-regulated debt collecting ([SRA](https://www.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/consumer-credit-activities/)). Construction payment is not one of the regulated claims-management sectors either ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/claims-management-regulation)). JobGuard's marketing should still avoid "claims" language, because claims firms had their fees capped after charging up to 40% ([MoneySavingExpert](https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/news/2022/03/claim-management-firms-now-have-fees-capped-by-fca/)).
- **Consumer law on late extra bills.** The UK ban on hidden add-on fees applies to consumer sales, so JobGuard's charge to the builder is largely outside it ([Linklaters](https://www.linklaters.com/en/insights/blogs/linkingcompetition/2025/june/consumer-deep-dive_understanding-drip-pricing-under-the-dmcc)). The builder's late bill to a homeowner is a consumer sale, though. It is likely to be more disputed and harder to collect than extras billed in the normal flow, which shrinks both the builder's benefit and JobGuard's fee base. The regulator's appetite is real: its first fine under the new rules was **£4.2m** against AA/BSM driving schools ([TLT](https://www.tlt.com/insights-and-events/insight/cma-issues-first-fine-for-breach-of-consumer-law-under-the-dmcc-act-in-drip-pricing-case)).

## Recommendation: £29, £59 and £119, a first-job trial and an unchanged 10%

| Plan | Monthly (ex VAT) | Annual (two months free) | Jobs live at once (starting guess) | Users |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solo | £29 | £290 | Up to 3 | Unlimited |
| **Builder (the plan to steer people towards)** | **£59** | **£590** | Up to 10 | Unlimited |
| Firm | £119 | £1,190 | Up to 25 | Unlimited |
| Contractor | Priced on request | — | More than 25 | Unlimited |
| All plans | Quoting free forever. A 10% success fee applies only to qualifying recovered money, on the same terms for every plan. | | | |

The reasoning for each part is as follows.

- **Why £29 for Solo.** It matches YourTradebase, Qted Solo and Powered Now's £28, sits just under Tradify's £34, and keeps the first-digit advantage over £30. Going higher would push against Checkatrade's free tool, the 29% digital adoption rate and a second compulsory tax subscription. Going much lower would make JobGuard look like a gadget.
- **Why £59 for Builder.** It is ServiceM8's middle price, cheaper than any per-user rival for a team of three, and roughly double the entry tier, which is the spacing the compromise effect needs.
- **Why £119 rather than £99 for Firm.** This is the main change to the founder's test points. A 10-person firm pays Tradify **£370–£440** a month, and a 20-person firm pays up to **£880** on Tradify Plus. A £99 ceiling would give that value away, and it anchors the middle plan weakly. £119 matches ServiceM8's Premium step and lets the "premium" instinct operate where buyers are least price-sensitive relative to their spend. The "Contractor" line captures the rare larger firm without a fourth tier to choose between.
- **Where premium should show.** Keep the prices steady. Avoid Fergus-style "75% off" introductory offers ([DashLink](https://dashlink.media/tradify-alternatives-uk/)). Let the evidence behind each find and the "we only earn when you're paid" fee carry the quality message.
- **The trial.** Run the watchdog and final account free on the first live job, with a ceiling of about 90 days, and ask for a card when the builder switches that job to live. Charge the success fee normally on that job, so the builder experiences the real model rather than learning that the fee is negotiable.
- **The 10% fee.** Keep it. It is already at the floor of the benchmarks, cutting it would buy little goodwill, and raising it would move JobGuard into the price range of claims firms.

The confidence behind these choices varies:

| Claim | Confidence | Why |
|---|---|---|
| UK trade software costs £25–£45 per user | High | Several vendor pages, checked September 2026 |
| No small-builder competitor offers a final check, an overcharge check or a success fee | Moderate | Based on pricing and feature pages only; Simpro could not be read |
| 10% is low against success-fee benchmarks | Moderate | Mostly industry or vendor sources; the FCA cap is solid |
| A higher price signals quality for JobGuard | Low | Consumer lab studies only; effect small and declining |
| The compromise effect favours a middle "hero" plan | Moderate | Consumer research; no test on software pricing pages |
| A trial tied to the first job beats a 14-day trial | Low to moderate | Field experiments exist, but not in trade software |
| How much builders lose to unbilled extras and supplier errors | Unknown | No robust UK data |

Several prices also remain unverified. Simpro, Commusoft and BigChange figures are third-party. Jobber and Joist bill only in US dollars. Fergus's pricing unit is disputed. WorkflowMax's £5.50–£17 per user may be a promotion ([WorkflowMax](https://www.workflowmax.com/pricing)). TradeFlow and TradeMore were seen only in search summaries.

The first real-user pilot is no-charge and cannot be billed after the fact. It will therefore measure usefulness, not willingness to pay. Before any charging starts, the founder should run structured price conversations with 10–20 builders and a priced sign-up page test (£29 against £39 for Solo, and a card at switch-live against no card), and should measure recovered money per job during the pilot to size the fee. The final choices then belong in the plan's commercial decision records (D09 for the subscription, alongside the fee policy) before anyone is charged. This report is evidence for that decision, not an approval of it.

## Conclusion

The research turns the founder's pricing question around. JobGuard does not need a high subscription to look good, because it already has a more credible quality signal than any price: a fee it earns only when the builder gets paid, backed by evidence the builder can inspect. Treated that way, the subscription's job is simply to be unremarkable. It should sit at market rates, be flat per business, and stay easy to justify in a trade where only 29% of one-person firms use any software. The real competitive gap lies in the combination no one else offers, not in price: capture on site, a variation-based final account, and a second check after the bill.

The real pricing risk is not about levels at all. It is about trust. A 10% fee on money a builder feels was found for them will be welcomed. The same fee on money the builder believes was kept from them will be resented and talked about. Every design choice that keeps the first reading true is therefore also a pricing decision: fee-free logging by the builder, unclear matches counting in the builder's favour, the must-surface rules, and a plain one-sentence explanation of the design. Until the pilot shows what the final check actually recovers, the business should run on subscriptions and treat the fee as proof of value rather than as the plan for revenue.
