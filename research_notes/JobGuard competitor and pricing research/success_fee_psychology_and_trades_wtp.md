# Success-fee psychology, fairness of a "hold-back-then-reveal" design, and UK tradespeople's willingness to pay for software

Research date: 2026-09-24. Scope: behavioural-economics evidence on (a) a 10% success fee on money a vendor finds or recovers, and (b) a design where the vendor withholds findings of missed billable extras until after the builder's final bill, then reveals them and takes 10% of what is recovered; plus UK data on tradespeople's software spend and adoption, and on revenue lost to unbilled extras and late payment.

Evidence-quality labels used below:
- **[Peer-reviewed]**: academic journal article.
- **[Gov/official]**: UK government, regulator or statutory body.
- **[Trade body]**: FMB, CIOB, FSB and similar.
- **[Vendor/marketing]**: a software, insurance or payments vendor's survey or blog. Treat as weak: often commissioned, small samples, methodology not published.
- **[Prior knowledge]**: a classic paper cited from the researcher's background knowledge with a DOI link. It was not re-fetched in this session.

## 1. Loss aversion and framing: "money you're losing" vs "money saved"

### Takeaway
Loss aversion is well established, and one field quasi-experiment with auditors (the closest analogue to a "watchdog" product) found that loss framing raised uptake of recommendations. Large meta-analyses, however, show that gain-vs-loss message framing effects on persuasion are very small on average. "You're losing £X" copy may help at the margin, but it is not a strong lever, and I found no robust SME/B2B-specific framing evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Prospect theory: losses loom larger than equivalent gains. The value function is concave for gains and convex for losses, and steeper for losses — [Kahneman & Tversky 1979, Econometrica, DOI 10.2307/1914185](https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185) [Prior knowledge]. The framing of the same outcomes as gains or losses reverses choices ("Asian disease" problem) — [Tversky & Kahneman 1981, Science, DOI 10.1126/science.7455683](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683) [Prior knowledge].
- Home energy auditors trained to (a) communicate vividly, (b) personalise recommendations, (c) induce commitment and (d) frame recommendations as "loss" rather than "gain" produced customers who reported greater likelihood of acting, and more applications to utility retrofit-finance programmes (telephone interviews and utility records). This is a quasi-experiment with nine auditors, and loss framing was bundled with three other techniques, so its separate effect is not isolated — [Gonzales, Aronson & Costanzo 1988, J. Applied Social Psychology 18:1049–1066](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1559-1816.1988.tb01192.x) [Peer-reviewed].
- Meta-analysis of 53 studies (N = 9,145): for disease-detection behaviours, loss-framed messages were only slightly more persuasive (r = −.04). A companion meta-analysis of 93 studies (N = 21,656) on prevention behaviours found gain frames slightly more persuasive (r = .03), mostly driven by dental-hygiene studies — [O'Keefe & Jensen 2009, Journal of Communication](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2009.01417.x); [O'Keefe & Jensen 2007, J. Health Communication](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10810730701615198) [Peer-reviewed].
- Vendor-blog claims that "81% of B2B buyers would consider value-based pricing if they could see the connection to outcomes" (attributed to Forrester) and "43% of enterprise buyers value vendor risk-sharing" (attributed to PwC) appear without primary links — [Schematic / Monetizely-type blogs via search summary](https://schematichq.com/blog/outcome-based-pricing) [Vendor/marketing; primary sources not located; do not cite as fact].

### Inferences
- The "auditor who shows you your losses" pattern (Gonzales et al.) is structurally close to JobGuard's final-check reveal. Vividness, personalisation (this job, this photo, this supplier invoice) and commitment are probably as important as the loss frame itself.
- Because generic framing effects are small, the fairness and trust of the mechanism (sections 3–4) will likely matter more to acceptance than copywriting.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study of loss vs gain framing aimed at small construction firms or UK SMEs buying software.
- The Forrester/PwC statistics on outcome-based pricing could not be traced to primary reports.

## 2. Mental accounting: windfalls, netting a fee from found money, decoupling payment from consumption

### Takeaway
Mental-accounting theory predicts that a fee taken out of newly found money is less painful than a separate upfront charge. Two mechanisms support this: losses are "cancelled against larger gains", and unanticipated money is treated as a windfall and spent more freely. That advantage depends on the fee being netted against the recovery at the moment of the gain. A fee invoiced separately and later, alongside a recurring subscription, re-creates a distinct "loss" and gives up much of the benefit.

### Cited Findings
- Thaler's hedonic-framing principles: segregate gains, integrate losses, **cancel (integrate) small losses against larger gains**, segregate "silver linings". The gain function is concave and the loss function convex, so a small loss absorbed into a larger gain hurts less than the same loss experienced alone — [Thaler 1985, "Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice", Marketing Science (PDF)](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/thaler-mktsci1985.pdf) [Peer-reviewed]; summary in [Mental accounting, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_accounting).
- Windfall gains are spent more readily than anticipated income, and the *unanticipated* nature of the money is what drives this. In two cash experiments, participants not forewarned of payment bet more and spent more than those who expected it — [Arkes et al. 1994, "The psychology of windfall gains", OBHDP 59:331–347](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597884710636) [Peer-reviewed]. A Registered Report replication and extension of Arkes et al.'s Studies 1–3 exists — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381575077_Revisiting_the_psychology_of_windfall_gains_Replication_and_extensions_Registered_Report_of_Arkes_et_al_1994%27s_Studies_1_2_and_3) (results not extracted in this session).
- "House money": after a prior gain, people take more risk with that money — [Thaler & Johnson 1990, Management Science, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.36.6.643](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.36.6.643) [Prior knowledge]; related recent work in [J. Risk and Uncertainty 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11166-023-09411-5).
- "The Red and the Black": a double-entry mental-accounting model with a *pain of paying*. Hedonically, the ideal is that payment is tightly coupled to consumption (paying evokes the benefit being financed) while consumption is decoupled from payment. The model predicts a preference for prepayment and flat-rate pricing, and a dislike of "taxi-meter" pay-per-use — [Prelec & Loewenstein 1998, Marketing Science 17(1):4–28 (CMU PDF)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/redblack.pdf) [Peer-reviewed].
- Transaction decoupling: bundling payments (e.g. a four-day ski pass vs four daily tickets) weakens the link between cost and benefit, so people attend less to what they paid and use less of what they bought — [Soman & Gourville 2001, JMR 38(1):30–44](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.38.1.30.18828) [Peer-reviewed].
- Flat-rate bias: consumers often choose flat-rate tariffs even when pay-per-use would be cheaper, partly for "insurance" and "taximeter" reasons — [Lambrecht & Skiera 2006, JMR, DOI 10.1509/jmkr.43.2.212](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.2.212) [Prior knowledge].

### Inferences
- **(a) The 10% success fee:** the psychologically cheapest form is "you recovered £1,000; £900 is yours" at the moment cash arrives (integrate the loss with the gain). A separate JobGuard invoice for £100 weeks later is closer to an isolated loss, especially if the builder has already mentally spent the £1,000.
- **The hold-back design deliberately turns found money into a windfall.** Arkes et al. imply that unanticipated money feels like "free" money, so a fee on it may be tolerated. The same research also implies that the windfall feeling depends on the builder not having expected the money. If the builder learns the product could have told them earlier, the money is no longer a windfall but *their own money that was held back*, and the fee turns into a toll (see section 4).
- **Subscription plus success fee:** Prelec & Loewenstein and flat-rate-bias evidence suggest tradespeople will tolerate a flat monthly subscription better than metered charges. A success fee is metered, but it is coupled to a visible benefit, which partly offsets the taxi-meter effect. The rev 2.3 design (no subscription offset against the fee) means the builder pays twice for one product. That is defensible, but the builder has to see a clear split of what each payment buys.
- Soman & Gourville suggest that a bundled subscription dulls attention to the tool over time. That is a churn risk: builders may stop noticing the value of a subscription they already pay for unless each month shows visible results.

### Gaps
- No study found of netted vs separately invoiced contingency fees for small businesses.
- No evidence found on whether tradespeople mentally treat late-recovered extras as a "windfall" or as "money I was always owed". This is a candidate for founder interviews and pilot testing.

## 3. Contingency / "no win, no fee" and pay-for-performance: acceptance and perceived fairness of the percentage

### Takeaway
UK benchmarks for contingency fees on recovered money cluster at **15–30%**. The FCA caps claims-management fees at 15–30% depending on claim size, and PPI at 20%; R&D tax advisers typically charge 10–30%. By comparison, **10% is low and likely to read as reasonable as a number.** Price-fairness research, however, says fairness depends on the perceived split of cost, effort and responsibility, not just the rate. In contingency markets the provider does the recovery work. In JobGuard's case the builder did the building, raises the invoice and collects the cash, so "10% for noticing" can look like a claim on the builder's own labour. Contingency models in the UK also carry reputational baggage (claims management companies, R&D boutiques), and regulators capped them because some fees were excessive.

### Cited Findings
- Dual entitlement: in telephone surveys, the public judged it fair for firms to raise prices when their costs rise, but unfair to exploit shifts in demand or market power (e.g. raising snow-shovel prices after a blizzard). Fairness is judged against a *reference transaction* to which both parties feel entitled — [Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1986, AER 76(4):728–741 (MIT PDF)](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Justice/Kahneman.pdf) [Peer-reviewed]; see also [Marketing Letters, "All's not fair in pricing"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00436145).
- Price-fairness framework: transaction-level fairness judgements depend on the *distribution of costs and profits* between buyer and seller and on *attributions of responsibility* for the price. Buyer–seller relationships and social or contextual factors moderate those judgements. Perceived unfairness produces negative emotions and actions such as complaint, exit and revenge — [Xia, Monroe & Cox 2004, J. Marketing 68(4):1–15](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.68.4.1.42733) [Peer-reviewed].
- UK regulated contingency benchmarks: **PPI claims-management fees are capped at 20% of the amount recovered** (set by Parliament). For other financial-services claims, CMCs may take **15–30% or £420–£10,000, whichever is lower**, depending on the size band — [FCA press release](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-launches-claims-management-companies-fees-cap); [FCA Handbook CMCOB 5](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/cmcob5) [Gov/official]. Before the cap, fees of up to 40% were common. The cap was expected to save consumers about £9.6m a year, and CMCs must explain how fees are calculated and point out free routes to redress — [MoneySavingExpert summary of FCA rules](https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/news/2022/03/claim-management-firms-now-have-fees-capped-by-fca/). The FCA also commissioned consumer research on CMC fee rules — [IFF Research for FCA (PDF)](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research/iff-research-claims-management-companies-cmc-fee-rules-research-financial-services-claims.pdf) (not extracted in this session).
- UK R&D tax-credit advisers: contingent fees typically range from 10% to 30% of the relief obtained. Industry commentators have raised concerns that contingent fees incentivise poor conduct, and that unregulated boutiques charge "eye-watering" fees. One commentator argued that fees of 20%+ are inappropriate when an engagement fee is also charged — [Source Advisors](https://sourceadvisors.co.uk/insights/latest-thinking/r-and-d-tax-relief/the-evolution-of-contingent-fees-and-their-place-in-rd-tax-credits-claims/); [Myriad Associates](https://www.myriadassociates.com/resources/news/2025/how-much-should-an-rd-tax-credit-claim-cost-in-2025/); [ForrestBrown](https://forrestbrown.co.uk/news/r-and-d-tax-credit-fees/) [Vendor/marketing: advisers with their own fee positions]. A US adviser cites 25–40% as "standard" there — [Swanson Reed](https://www.swansonreed.com/research-tax-credit/faqs/what-is-the-standard-contingency-fee-for-rd-tax-credit-advisors/) [Vendor/marketing, US].
- Outcome-based pricing is still rare in software. A "2025 State of B2B Monetization" report is quoted as saying it is out of reach for 95% of the market, mainly because outcomes are hard to define and attribute — [Schematic blog](https://schematichq.com/blog/outcome-based-pricing); [L.E.K. Consulting](https://www.lek.com/insights/saas-pricing/rise-outcome-based-pricing-saas-aligning-value-cost) [Vendor/consultancy].
- Operational transparency: when a service shows the work it is doing, people value it more, even preferring longer waits with identical results. Across five experiments, perceived effort induced *reciprocity*, which mediated the higher valuation — [Buell & Norton 2011, Management Science 57(9):1564–1579 (HBS PDF)](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Norton_Michael_The%20labor%20illusion%20How%20operational_f4269b70-3732-4fc4-8113-72d0c47533e0.pdf) [Peer-reviewed].

### Inferences
- **10% is well under UK contingency norms (15–30%)**, so the headline rate is unlikely to be the main objection. A shorthand for the pitch could be "half what a claims firm takes". This is an inference: I found no builder-specific test of it.
- **Attribution is the fairness crux (Xia et al.).** In CMC and R&D markets the provider does the claim and bears the effort, so the fee pays for work. For a missed extra, the builder did the physical work, and JobGuard's contribution is detection. Builders are likely to accept a fee where JobGuard evidently did work they could not easily do: matching supplier invoices, photos and messages across a whole job. They are likely to reject it where the extra was already in their head or notes. This supports rev 2.3's rule that builder capture is always fee-free and that ambiguity resolves in the builder's favour.
- **Operational transparency can legitimise the fee.** Showing the evidence trail (which photo, which supplier line, which date) should raise perceived effort and reciprocity (Buell & Norton). The rev 2.3 evidence-pack approach is consistent with this.
- **Reputational association:** "no win, no fee" in the UK is tied to claims-management firms and R&D boutiques, both regulated or criticised for excessive fees and poor conduct. Positioning language should probably avoid "claims" and "recovery agent" connotations.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on small businesses' perceived fairness of *specific percentages* for success fees.
- The FCA/IFF consumer research on CMC fees was not extracted. It might contain direct evidence on how consumers judge 20–30% fees.

## 4. Trust and fairness risks of a vendor profiting from withholding information

### Takeaway
This is the highest-risk element. The literature consistently finds that: (1) people are especially averse to losses caused by *another party's* choices rather than by chance (betrayal aversion); (2) profiting from an information or power advantage is judged unfair (dual entitlement); (3) disclosing a conflict of interest does not neutralise it and can make advisers behave worse; and (4) hidden or late-revealed charges provoke strong regulatory and consumer backlash in the UK. The UK drip-pricing ban covers consumer transactions, so the JobGuard-to-builder relationship (B2B) is largely outside it. But the builder-to-homeowner invoice for a late-revealed extra *is* a consumer transaction, which creates a second-order risk.

### Cited Findings
- Betrayal aversion: people demand a higher probability of a good outcome before trusting a person than before taking an equivalent lottery. The mean minimum acceptable probability was 54% in a trust game vs 37% in a lottery; people take risks less willingly when the source of uncertainty is another person rather than nature — [Bohnet & Zeckhauser 2004, "Trust, Risk and Betrayal" (SSRN)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=447660). Replicated in six countries, with a 10–20 point differential in the US, Switzerland and Brazil — [Bohnet et al. 2008, AER](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.98.1.294) [Peer-reviewed]. A later paper questions how far betrayal aversion guides trust towards strangers — [Fetchenhauer et al. 2020, JBDM](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/bdm.2166).
- Disclosing a conflict of interest has perverse effects. Recipients under-discount advice even when the conflict is disclosed, and advisers who disclose feel morally licensed and strategically encouraged to exaggerate further — [Cain, Loewenstein & Moore 2005, J. Legal Studies 34(1):1–25 (SSRN)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=480121); follow-up [Loewenstein, Cain & Sah, "When sunlight fails to disinfect" (CMU PDF)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WhenSunLightFails.pdf); a direct replication exists in the [J. Economic Psychology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167487018302964) (results not extracted) [Peer-reviewed].
- Dual entitlement: exploiting an advantage to raise profit is judged unfair, while passing on cost increases is judged fair — [Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1986](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Justice/Kahneman.pdf) [Peer-reviewed].
- Operational transparency increases perceived value through reciprocity — [Buell & Norton 2011](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=40158) [Peer-reviewed]. By implication, deliberately *opaque* operation during the job removes that reciprocity channel until the reveal.
- UK regulatory mood: the DMCC Act 2024 bans drip pricing from 6 April 2025. Drip pricing (showing an initial price and adding mandatory fees later) is unfair in all circumstances, and headline prices must include fixed mandatory charges — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/no-hidden-charges-clamping-down-on-drip-pricing); [Taylor Wessing on CMA guidance](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/04/dmcca-drip-pricing) [legal commentary]. The CMA can fine up to £300,000 or 10% of worldwide turnover directly. Its first DMCC fine was £4.2m plus >£760,000 in refunds against AA/BSM Driving Schools for drip pricing — [TLT](https://www.tlt.com/insights-and-events/insight/cma-issues-first-fine-for-breach-of-consumer-law-under-the-dmcc-act-in-drip-pricing-case); [Skadden 2026](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/aa-and-bsm-driving-schools). Three further drip-pricing investigations were opened in July 2026 — [HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/crt/2026-07/cma-launches-three-further-drip-pricing-investigations-under-the-dmcc-act-consumer-protection-regime).
- **Scope:** pure B2B sales are largely outside the DMCC consumer regime, and the drip-pricing rules apply to consumer transactions — [Linklaters](https://www.linklaters.com/en/insights/blogs/linkingcompetition/2025/june/consumer-deep-dive_understanding-drip-pricing-under-the-dmcc); [Charles Russell Speechlys 2026](https://www.charlesrussellspeechlys.com/en/insights/expert-insights/commercial/2026/drip-pricing-and-enforcement-how-the-dmcc-act-is-changing-the-rules/). Note that many builders are sole traders, and the consumer/trader boundary rests on acting "for purposes relating to trade", so a builder buying JobGuard for the business is a trader.
- Tradespeople often choose not to bill small extras. In a Simply Business survey, **34% receive no payment for extra work**, and one quoted tradesperson does extras free "under the proviso that I might get a more positive review". 62% say helping beyond the job has led to recommendations and 27% to better reviews. 19% say customers now expect extras as standard — [Simply Business, June 2026, n = 408](https://www.simplybusiness.co.uk/knowledge/trades/unpaid-tasks-costing-uk-tradespeople/) [Vendor/marketing: insurer-commissioned].

### Inferences
- **Discovery risk dominates.** If a builder learns that JobGuard "knew on day 12 and told me on day 60 so it could take 10%", betrayal aversion and dual entitlement predict a strong, possibly permanent, trust loss that spreads by word of mouth in a referral-driven trade. A written disclosure of the design (Cain et al.) is unlikely to remove the objection and may simply be discounted.
- **The fairest-looking framing is a timing rationale that benefits the builder,** such as "an independent second check after you've done your own bill, like a proofread". The mechanisms in rev 2.3 help make that framing credible: capture is always fee-free, builder-favoured matching, permanent ineligibility for anything disclosed early, and must-surface safety and dispute rules (AGENTS §5.14). The framing becomes untenable if any path lets the product *know and stay silent* about something the builder needed during the job. For example, a customer-agreed variation that needed prior written agreement is covered by the D13 must-surface rules only if those rules are broad in practice.
- **Deliberate goodwill:** some unbilled extras are *chosen* goodwill (Simply Business). A reveal that implicitly tells the builder to chase the homeowner for them, plus a fee if they do, can feel like pressure against the builder's own customer-relationship judgement. The design should treat "I chose not to charge this" as a first-class, fee-free outcome, not a lost fee.
- **Second-order consumer-law risk (inference; needs legal review):** a builder issuing a homeowner an additional bill *after* the final account, for extras never priced or agreed in advance, is a B2C transaction. It may attract complaints of surprise charges, and depending on the contract and the Consumer Rights Act 2015, the builder may only be able to claim a reasonable price for work not priced in advance. Late-reveal recoveries may therefore be harder to collect and more dispute-prone than extras billed in the normal flow, which lowers both the builder's realised benefit and JobGuard's fee base.
- **Moral hazard perception:** the pattern resembles the R&D-adviser and CMC markets, where contingent fees were criticised for incentivising poor conduct (section 3). Critics or journalists could frame a watchdog that profits from what the builder missed as rewarding the vendor for *not* helping earlier. The per-job lock, evidence-before-lock rule and the absence of any fee on builder capture are the main defences. They should be explainable in one sentence.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found of a vendor that *intentionally delays* disclosure to create a fee base and how customers respond. Closest analogues are CMC and R&D contingency markets and conflict-of-interest disclosure studies.
- The CMA drip-pricing guidance addresses consumers. I found no UK authority on B2B "delayed-disclosure" fee designs. Unfair-terms and misrepresentation risk in B2B contracts was not researched and needs a lawyer.
- The Consumer Rights Act point above is an inference and was not sourced in this session.

## 5. Willingness to pay and software adoption among UK tradespeople and SMEs

### Takeaway
Construction is the *least* digitised sector among UK one-person businesses: 29% use web-based tools or software, against 53% of all no-employee businesses. Mainstream UK job-management software is priced at roughly **£34–£44 per user per month** (Tradify list prices), and accounting software starts at about £12–£20 a month. Two structural shifts are relevant from 2026. Making Tax Digital for Income Tax is forcing software onto sole traders above £50k (2026), falling to £30k (2027) and £20k (2028). Free AI job-management apps (Checkatrade TradeMore) are putting downward pressure on subscription prices. Claims that tradespeople spend "£200–£400 a month across eight tools" are vendor marketing and unverified.

### Cited Findings
- **DBT Longitudinal Small Business Survey 2024 (businesses with no employees, n = 2,192, fieldwork Oct 2024–May 2025):** 53% used technologies or web-based software to sell or manage the business, up 9 points on 2023. **Construction was lowest at 29%**; information and communications was highest at 73%. Among no-employee businesses, 49% used accountancy software (vs 80% of SME employers) and 41% e-invoicing (vs 62%). For record-keeping, 54% used paper, 52% spreadsheets and 32% software — [GOV.UK LSBS 2024: businesses with no employees](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/small-business-survey-2024-businesses-with-no-employees/longitudinal-small-business-survey-2024-businesses-with-no-employees) [Gov/official].
- **Making Tax Digital for Income Tax** began 6 April 2026 for sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000. HMRC estimates 860,000 are affected in the first wave, with the threshold falling to £30,000 in 2027 and £20,000 in 2028. Quarterly updates must be filed via compatible software — [Wikipedia: Making Tax Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Making_Tax_Digital); [GoCardless guide](https://gocardless.com/blog/mtd-itsa-sole-traders-landlords-2026-guide). Sage/IPSE research found 39% of sole traders had not started preparing — [IPSE–Sage MTD report (PDF)](https://ipse.imgix.net/downloads/RESEARCH/IPSE-Sage-MTD-Report.pdf); [cfotech summary](https://cfotech.co.uk/story/uk-sole-traders-unready-for-making-tax-digital-shift) [Trade body + vendor].
- **Tradify UK list prices (fetched 2026-09-24):** Lite £34, Pro £37, Plus £44 per user per month, plus Custom (contact sales). Add-ons: Instant Website £10/month, SMS £0.10/message. The page does not say whether VAT is included — [Tradify UK pricing](https://www.tradifyhq.com/uk/pricing) [Vendor list price, primary].
- Comparison blogs put job-management tools (Tradify, ServiceM8, Powered Now, Jobber) at roughly £30–£80/month and say most sole traders and small teams pay £15–£40/month. They cite QuickBooks from about £12/month and Xero from about £19.50/month — [TradePlanr price guide](https://tradeplanr.com/blog/how-much-does-job-management-software-cost); [brcks.io shortlist](https://www.brcks.io/blog/job-management-apps/) [Vendor/marketing: competitors writing about competitors].
- Claim: "UK tradespeople report paying £200–£400 monthly across multiple tools" and "juggling eight different platforms" — surfaced via [Kwowta / Checker / Elec Training-type blogs](https://kwowta.com/best-apps-running-trades-business-uk-2026/) [Vendor/marketing; source survey not identified; **do not rely on**].
- Claim: "UK Admin Drain Report 2026" says trades business owners lose about 8 hours a week to admin (384 hours a year; about £17,000 of billable time at £45/hour) — [surfaced via search on itrade/linktly-type blogs](https://www.itrade.net/post/the-best-tradify-alternatives-for-uk-trade-businesses-2026) [Vendor/marketing; methodology unknown].
- **Checkatrade launched TradeMore**, a *free* AI job-management app open to all UK tradespeople, not just Checkatrade members. Tradify, Powered Now, ServiceM8 and BigChange are also pushing AI features — [Kaizen AI Consulting summary](https://kaizenaiconsulting.com/checkatrade-trademore-free-ai-job-management/) [secondary blog; the launch should be verified on Checkatrade's own site]. Wickes also markets AI tools to tradespeople — [Wickes Trade](https://www.wickes.co.uk/trade/ai-tools-for-tradespeople-summer-26).
- Subscription fatigue (consumer, not trade-specific): nearly two in five Brits have cancelled or cut subscriptions to save money, and 42% admit paying for unused subscriptions — [Just Pricing / Readless compilations](https://justpricing.com/subscription-economy-statistics) [aggregator; consumer data; weak for B2B trades].
- FMB/CIOB State of Trade 2025: SME builders' workloads were strong in H1 2025 (+25% balance, enquiries +34%), but skills shortages constrain delivery. Over 60% struggle to recruit skilled trades and 49% report job delays — [FMB State of Trade H1 2025](https://www.fmb.org.uk/resource/state-of-trade-h1-2025-building-momentum-but-challenges-remain.html); [CIOB H2 2025](https://www.ciob.org/industry/research/State-of-Trade-H2-2025) [Trade body]. These surveys did not surface software-spend data.

### Inferences
- **Price anchor:** a UK builder's reference price for "business software" is probably £15–£45 per user per month for job management, plus about £12–£30 for accounting (now effectively mandatory above £50k via MTD). A JobGuard subscription materially above about £40–£50/month would need a clearly separate value story, and the success fee would come on top of that.
- **The free competitor is a real threat to subscription WTP** (TradeMore). JobGuard's differentiator has to be the profit-watchdog and final-check function, which free job-management tools do not claim. The success-fee component is, psychologically, the part tied to proven value.
- **Low digital baseline:** at 29% adoption among one-person construction firms, many prospects are currently on paper or spreadsheets. MTD is the forcing event. Onboarding friction, not price, may be the binding constraint for the smallest firms.
- **Churn drivers (inference from mental-accounting section plus fatigue data):** a subscription whose value is invisible in a given month is at risk. The success-fee reveal creates visible value moments, but only at job completion.

### Gaps
- **No robust UK survey found on tradespeople's actual monthly software spend or stated WTP.** The widely quoted £200–£400/month figure could not be traced. FSB, Sage and Xero small-business reports were searched but did not yield a trade-specific software-spend statistic in this session.
- No UK churn data specific to trades software was found (vendors do not publish it).
- Construction-specific MTD readiness figures were not found.

## 6. How much revenue small builders lose to unbilled extras, late payment and supplier errors

### Takeaway
Late payment is well measured by the government: about **£11bn a year cost to the UK economy, about 14,000 business closures a year, and £26bn owed at any time** (DBT/OSBC, London Economics, July 2025). **Unbilled extras and variations are poorly measured.** The only recent UK figure found is an insurer-commissioned survey (Simply Business, n = 408) estimating about £2,600 a year of unpaid "whilst you're here" work, and its published arithmetic does not reconcile. **I found no robust UK data on revenue lost to supplier invoicing errors** for small builders.

### Cited Findings
- **Late payment (robust):** estimated to cost the UK economy almost **£11bn a year**, with about **14,000 business closures a year** (38 a day). About 1.5m businesses (28%) are affected each year, with **£26bn owed** at any time (average £17,000 per affected business). 22% spend staff time chasing, averaging 86 hours a year per affected business (133m hours economy-wide). Research by DBT and the Office of the Small Business Commissioner, carried out by London Economics and published 31 July 2025 — [Small Business Commissioner: Late Payments Research](https://www.smallbusinesscommissioner.gov.uk/late-payments-research-2/) [Gov/official]. FSB policy work on late payment — [FSB "Pay it Forward"](https://www.fsb.org.uk/resources/policy-reports/pay-it-forward-MCKC5VC53IXVAT7MMXYAU5M424RM) [Trade body; not extracted].
- LSBS 2024: **51% of no-employee businesses that give trade credit experienced late payment problems** (construction not separately reported) — [GOV.UK LSBS 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/small-business-survey-2024-businesses-with-no-employees/longitudinal-small-business-survey-2024-businesses-with-no-employees) [Gov/official].
- Construction subcontractors: a Payapps survey says 79% are "almost always paid late" and 73% experience routine payment-application disputes (n = 127) — [pbctoday](https://www.pbctoday.co.uk/news/building-control-news/uk-subcontractors-still-stung-by-constant-late-payments-report-finds/165884) [Vendor/marketing; small sample]. "Nine in ten subcontractors wait over 30 days" — [Building](https://www.building.co.uk/nine-in-ten-subcontractors-wait-over-30-days-for-payment/5071419.article) (details not extracted). A Billd figure of a 51-day average wait is a **US** report, so do not use it for the UK. Note that these are mostly subcontractor-to-main-contractor payments, not the domestic builder-to-homeowner payments most relevant to JobGuard.
- **Unbilled extras (weak):** 92% of tradespeople have been asked to do extra work beyond the booking; 62% say it happens regularly and 19% on almost every job; 1.8 hours a week of unpaid extra work; **34% receive no payment** for extras. The headline "£2,600 a year" is described as 1.8 h/week × 52 = 93.6 h × £30/h, but that equals £2,808, not £2,600, so **the arithmetic does not reconcile as published**. Commissioned by Simply Business (insurer), n = 408, May–June 2026 — [Simply Business](https://www.simplybusiness.co.uk/knowledge/trades/unpaid-tasks-costing-uk-tradespeople/) [Vendor/marketing].
- Commentary that uncontrolled scope changes often go unbilled and erode margins — [Access Group, construction profit margins](https://www.theaccessgroup.com/en-gb/construction/resources/construction-profit-margins-uk/) [Vendor/marketing; no quantified UK SME estimate].

### Inferences
- The robust, government-measured pain point is **late payment**, not unbilled extras. Marketing copy leaning on "£X billion lost to unbilled work" would currently have no robust UK source. Any figure used should be labelled as a vendor survey or estimated from JobGuard's own pilot data.
- The Simply Business data cuts both ways for JobGuard. It confirms that unpaid extras are common, but it also shows that a meaningful share are *deliberate* goodwill, which limits the recoverable pool and the fee base.
- Recoverable value per job for a typical small domestic builder is therefore unknown. The 10% fee's revenue potential cannot be sized from public data and needs pilot measurement.

### Gaps
- **No robust UK estimate found of revenue lost to unbilled variations or extras** by small builders. FMB State of Trade and House Builders surveys do not appear to measure it.
- **No UK data found on supplier invoicing errors** (overcharges, short deliveries) for small builders.
- No construction-specific cut of the DBT/London Economics late-payment figures was extracted.
